---
title: The character arc
---

A story is the account of someone changed by what they go through. The protagonist starts with a want they can name and a lack they cannot: a flaw, a false belief or a wound that shapes how they act. The plot presses on that flaw until the old way of coping stops working; the character either changes or is broken by refusing to.

What a reader feels as an arc is cause and effect inside a person. Each setback should trace back, at least in part, to the flaw — failure the character earns is failure the reader believes. The change must be paid for on the page, in a scene where the character chooses differently than they would have at the start, and it should show in all three lines of the story: the external goal, the inner struggle and the relationships the flaw strains.

It fails when the flaw is only described, when setbacks come from luck, when the change happens between chapters, or when the protagonist is carried by events rather than choosing.

A flat arc is legitimate: a character who does not change but changes the world around them. Then the pressure falls on what they believe, and the test is whether that belief is ever seriously threatened.
