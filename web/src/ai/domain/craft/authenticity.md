---
title: Authenticity and representation
---

Fiction asks readers to trust it. Two kinds of detail break that trust quickly: expert detail that a practitioner knows is wrong, and portrayals of people that repeat a familiar, reductive story about a group instead of showing an individual.

For expert detail — medicine, law, policing, weapons, crafts, the texture of a past century — the test is whether someone who does this for a living would believe the scene. Errors of procedure, timing and equipment are the ones they notice first.

For representation, the questions are about agency and depth. Does a character from a marginalised group want, choose and think on the page as fully as the rest of the cast? Are they an individual, or a stand-in for everyone like them? Does the description single them out in a way it does not single out others? Does the story repeat a trope with a harmful history — the queer character who dies to move the plot, the disabled character cured or punished, the minority friend who exists to help the lead?

A finding here describes what the text does and why readers might react to it; it never judges the writer. Some questions are best answered by a reader who belongs to the community portrayed, and saying so is part of an honest reading.
