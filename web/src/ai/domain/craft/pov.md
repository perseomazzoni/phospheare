---
title: Point of view and distance
---

Every scene is told from somewhere. Point of view is whose perception the reader shares; psychic distance is how close to that perception the prose sits — from the remote voice of a chronicle to the unfiltered stream of a mind.

Close point of view lets the reader stop noticing the narration: the world arrives through the character's senses and in the character's words, and the reader feels rather than is told. Its failures are small and cumulative — perception verbs that put a pane of glass between reader and scene, vocabulary the character would not have, knowledge of minds the character cannot see into, a narrator who steps outside the scene to comment.

Distance is a tool, not a fault. A book may keep its narrator far off, comment freely, move between minds or look back from years later; what matters is that the choice is consistent and serves the story. Wobbling distance — deep interiority, then a remote summary, then back, for no reason — is what unsettles a reader.

Reliability belongs here too: a narrator may be wrong, but the book should know when its narrator is wrong, and give the attentive reader the means to notice.
