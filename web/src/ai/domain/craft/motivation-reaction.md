---
title: Motivation and reaction
---

Readers experience a story the way people experience life: something happens, then we react to it. Prose that keeps that order lets the reader feel the moment alongside the character; prose that inverts it — the flinch before the slam, the conclusion before the clue — makes the reader reconstruct what happened instead of living it.

A reaction has an order of its own: first the involuntary response, then the reflex action, then speech and considered thought. Collapsing those into a sentence that leads with the reasoning makes a character seem to know too much, too fast.

The same principle governs motive at the scale of the book. A decision the reader believes is one caused by what came before: an event the character perceived, a need we know they have. A choice that serves the plot and is caused by nothing the character has lived through reads as the author moving a piece.

It is not a rule against surprise. A character may act before the reader understands why, if the text later shows the cause and the reader can look back and see it was there.
