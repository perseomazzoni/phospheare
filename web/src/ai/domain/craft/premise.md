---
title: The premise
---

Beneath its plot, a book argues something — a claim about people that the events put to the test: what jealousy does, what loyalty costs, whether a person can outrun their past. The premise is that claim in a sentence, with a subject, a pressure and an outcome.

A reader never needs to be told the premise, and should not be. They feel it as coherence: the subplots rhyme with the main line, the ending answers the question the opening raised, and scenes that seemed to be about something else turn out to be about the same thing.

It fails in two ways. A book with no premise is a sequence of events that stops rather than ends. A book with two premises that contradict each other — one subplot proving the opposite of the main line, by accident — leaves the reader unsure what they were told.

The test for a scene is simple: taken alone, what would it prove? If the answer fights the book's argument and the book does not know it, the scene is working against the whole. A deliberate counter-argument, answered by the ending, is how a book earns its conclusion.
