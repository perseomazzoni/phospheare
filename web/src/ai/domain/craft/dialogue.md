---
title: Dialogue
---

Dialogue is action. People speak to get something — reassurance, advantage, the truth, to be left alone — and the best exchanges are small struggles between wants. Talk that exists to inform the reader, with characters telling each other what both already know, stops the story to explain it.

Real speech is oblique. People deflect, answer the question they wish they had been asked, and say the opposite of what they feel. Subtext — the gap between the line and what it means — is where a reader does the pleasurable work of understanding. Characters who state their feelings exactly leave the reader nothing to do.

Attribution should be invisible. A plain tag disappears; a tag that tells the reader how to hear the line competes with it. An action beat can attribute a line and stage the scene at once, but beats that only nod, sigh and shrug are clutter.

Dialogue also needs a place. Long runs of lines with no body, room or gesture turn characters into voices in the dark. And each voice should be its own: cover the names and a reader should still know who is speaking, from vocabulary, rhythm and what each one notices.
