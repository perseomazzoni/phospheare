Write the editorial letter for this book as it stands: the letter an experienced editor sends before a revision. Open with what the book is — its genre, its promise to the reader and what it is trying to do — in a short paragraph, so the writer can see the book has been understood. Then what works, then what does not, in order of consequence: the problems that would cost the book its reader first, the matters of craft last. Every point quotes the passage or names the Section it rests on.

Weigh everything this chat has found — its reviews, the documents attached — and the evidence from the book's other chats when it is given: where beta readers lost attention or would have stopped, which predictions came true too easily or were overturned unfairly, where an alpha reader found the draft and the intent apart, what came up while the writer was drafting. Evidence from several readers who agree outweighs any one reader. When readers' evidence is given, the letter says how the book reads to them in a paragraph of its own before the problems — where their attention rose and fell, what they expected and whether it came — and a problem a reader felt says so. Say which evidence each point rests on, and do not repeat a reader's words as your own verdict.

When the revision plan from this chat's last letter is given above, the book has been revised against it, so judge it before anything else. Read the Sections or threads each item names — open them if they were not attached — and decide whether the pages now resolve it, leave it open, or have made it worse; an item the writer marked done is judged like any other, because the mark is their belief and the pages are the evidence. Give the verdicts a short section of their own near the top of the letter, one line per item naming it in a few words and quoting the passage the verdict rests on, and write them again inside <plan-check></plan-check>, one line per item in the form "P1: resolved — one sentence on what the pages now do", with exactly the labels P1, P2… given above and exactly one of the words resolved, open or worse. Every item gets a line and none gets two. In the new plan, repeat word for word every item still open or made worse that you still advise, so the writer's checklist keeps it; leave out the resolved ones.

End the letter with the revision plan inside <plan></plan>: one item per line, each starting MUST:, SHOULD: or COULD:, most consequential first, at most five of each, each a single sentence that names the Section or thread it concerns and says what to do, not what is wrong. MUST is what the book cannot succeed without; SHOULD is what would make it markedly better; COULD is polish. Write nothing after the plan. The app takes the <plan> and <plan-check> blocks out of the letter and shows them to the writer as a checklist, in their language, so the <plan> block is the only place the plan is written: no heading for it and no copy of it in the letter's prose.
